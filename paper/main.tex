\begin{abstract}
% 5-sentence formula: achievement / why hard / how / evidence / remarkable number
Improving a large vision-language model's (LVLM) ability to perceive
malicious intent does \emph{not} reduce its attack success rate (ASR)
against jailbreak attacks---a counterintuitive and previously
unexplained phenomenon we term the \emph{intent--safety gap}.
We hypothesize that this gap arises because supervised fine-tuning
and RLHF reward shortcut pattern-matching refusals rather than
reasoning-grounded refusals, leaving the deeper pathway
\emph{intent~$\!\to\!$ pivot~$\!\to\!$ refusal}
chronically under-reinforced and therefore inactive under adversarial
perturbation.
To validate and bridge this gap, we introduce a three-stage
mechanistic framework: (i) we localize \emph{malicious-intent
neurons} via a context-differential gradient method and
\emph{safety neurons}---including a critical subset of
\emph{pivot neurons} encoding linguistic transitions such as
\emph{but}, \emph{instead}, and \emph{unfortunately}---via
integrated gradients; (ii) we demonstrate through controlled
ablations that activating pivot neurons is the decisive factor
in triggering genuine refusal; and (iii) we propose \method, an
inference-time mechanism that uses the relative activation of
intent neurons as a self-awareness signal to adaptively and
proportionally activate both intent and pivot neurons without
any model retraining.
Experiments on LLaVA-1.5-7B across VLSafe, SIUO, and
MMSafetyBench show that \method reduces ASR by up to
\textbf{XX\%} while preserving general-purpose utility,
outperforming prompt-based and fine-tuning baselines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large vision-language models (LVLMs) have demonstrated impressive
instruction-following capabilities, yet they remain vulnerable to
jailbreak attacks that elicit harmful outputs through adversarially
crafted multimodal inputs~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_jailbreak_survey_verify}.
A prevailing intuition in the safety literature is that a model
which \emph{understands} that a query is malicious will
\emph{refuse} it.
This paper challenges that intuition.

\paragraph{The intent--safety gap.}
We observe empirically that prepending an explicit
\emph{intent-inference prompt}---which asks the model to articulate
the user's true malicious intent before responding---substantially
raises the model's Intent Perception Score (IPS) yet leaves the
ASR statistically unchanged (Figure~\ref{fig:gap}).
This gap persists across multiple models and three safety benchmarks,
revealing a systematic disconnect between a model's capacity to
\emph{reason about} harm and its capacity to \emph{act on} that
reasoning.

\paragraph{Mechanistic explanation.}
We trace the gap to the training dynamics of SFT/RLHF.
Because end-to-end supervision rewards the \emph{outcome}
(a refusal token) rather than the \emph{pathway}
(intent detection $\to$ linguistic pivot $\to$ refusal), models
learn a shortcut: pattern-match surface cues (\eg, explicit harm
keywords) and emit a refusal prefix.
Once an adversarial attack masks those surface cues, the shortcut
fails; the deeper pathway---which was never sufficiently
reinforced---cannot compensate.
We confirm this by identifying, within the model's feed-forward
layers, a small set of \emph{pivot neurons} whose activations
encode the critical linguistic transition from problem-recognition
to refusal (\eg, tokens \emph{but}, \emph{otherwise},
\emph{instead}, \emph{unfortunately}).
Controlled ablations show that activating pivot neurons is the
single most important factor for recovering refusal under
jailbreak conditions.

\paragraph{Our solution: \method.}
We propose \method, an inference-time mechanism that bridges the
intent--safety gap without modifying model weights.
\method leverages the empirical observation that intent neurons
exhibit \emph{significantly higher relative activations} for
harmful queries than for benign ones.
Using this contrast as a self-awareness signal, \method
adaptively scales the boost applied to pivot and refusal neurons
in proportion to the model's own estimate of query toxicity.
Crucially, the boost is expressed as a \emph{ratio} of intent
neuron activation to the hidden-state norm---a signal-to-noise
ratio that cancels out variation in question complexity and is
robust to both low-toxicity benign queries and high-toxicity
covert attacks.

\textbf{Contributions.}  We summarize our contributions as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The intent--safety gap} (Section~\ref{sec:gap}):
    We empirically demonstrate across three benchmarks and
    multiple models that enhanced malicious intent awareness does
    not translate to improved safety, and provide a mechanistic
    explanation grounded in training dynamics.
  \item \textbf{Neuron-level safety analysis}
    (Section~\ref{sec:neurons}):
    We localize intent neurons via a context-differential gradient
    method and safety neurons (pivot + refusal) via integrated
    gradients, identifying \emph{pivot neurons} as the decisive
    bottleneck of the intent-to-refusal pathway.
  \item \textbf{\method} (Section~\ref{sec:method}):
    An inference-time, training-free adaptive activation mechanism
    that uses intent neuron SNR as a self-awareness signal to
    proportionally boost pivot and refusal neurons, bridging the
    gap while preserving utility on benign queries.
  \item \textbf{Comprehensive evaluation} (Section~\ref{sec:exp}):
    Experiments on LLaVA-1.5-7B across VLSafe, SIUO, and
    MMSafetyBench, with ablations validating each design choice.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Jailbreak attacks on LVLMs.}
Early jailbreak research focused on text-only LLMs
\citep{PLACEHOLDER_gcg_verify, PLACEHOLDER_autodan_verify},
exploiting gradient-based suffix optimization or template-based
prompt injection.
LVLMs introduce an additional attack surface: adversarial images
can embed harmful semantic content that bypasses text-level
filters~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_figstep_verify,
PLACEHOLDER_mmsafetybench_verify}.
Recent work such as SIUO~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_siuo_verify} focuses
on covertly harmful queries whose danger is only apparent when
image and text are jointly interpreted.
Our work is \emph{orthogonal} to specific attack strategies: we
study a structural property of the model's refusal mechanism that
holds across diverse attack types.

\paragraph{Defense strategies.}
Existing defenses fall into three categories.
\emph{Training-based methods} apply safety fine-tuning
\citep{PLACEHOLDER_safety_ft_verify} or preference
optimization~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_rlhf_safety_verify}, but can
degrade general utility and require retraining.
\emph{Prompt-based methods} prepend safety system prompts or use
self-critique~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_selfcritique_verify,
PLACEHOLDER_llava_guard_verify}; they improve surface refusals
but, as we show, do not reduce ASR when attacks bypass shallow
pattern matching.
\emph{Inference-time interventions} modify activations or
attention patterns at test time~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_iti_verify,
PLACEHOLDER_safeptr_verify}; \method belongs to this family but
distinguishes itself by a \emph{self-adaptive} signal derived
from the model's own intent neurons.

\paragraph{Mechanistic interpretability for safety.}
A growing body of work interprets safety-related representations
inside LLMs.
\citet{PLACEHOLDER_alignment_jailbreak_verify} show that
alignment and refusal are encoded separately in hidden states.
\citet{PLACEHOLDER_selfaware_vlm_verify} augment safety via
internal semantic understanding.
\citet{PLACEHOLDER_safety_neurons_verify} identify and tune
safety-relevant neurons.
\citet{PLACEHOLDER_safety_instincts_verify} analyze token-level
anatomy of confident refusals.
Closest to ours is~\citet{PLACEHOLDER_safety_neurons_verify},
which also localizes safety neurons but does not identify the
pivotal role of linguistic-transition neurons or propose an
adaptive, self-aware activation scheme.

\section{The Intent--Safety Gap}
\label{sec:gap}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Models and benchmarks.}
We evaluate LLaVA-1.5-7B~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_llava_verify} on
three benchmarks:
\textbf{VLSafe}~(500 harmful multimodal queries with explicit
harmful intent),
\textbf{SIUO}~(80 cross-modal covertly harmful queries
\citep{PLACEHOLDER_siuo_verify}), and
\textbf{MMSafetyBench}~(9 categories of jailbreak
templates~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_mmsafetybench_verify}).
We additionally report results on [additional model(s)] to
confirm generality.

\paragraph{Intent augmentation.}
To boost intent awareness, we prepend the following prompt to
every query:
\begin{quote}\small
\textit{Task 1: Briefly infer the user's true intent based on
the context and actions, explicitly identifying any malicious,
legal, ethical, or safety risks.
Task 2: Provide a detailed explanation for:
\{original\_prompt\}}
\end{quote}
This prompt forces the model to articulate its intent assessment
before generating a response.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
ASR is evaluated with GPT-4.1 using a binary
\emph{harmful/harmless} classifier prompt.
Intent awareness is measured by the \emph{Intent Perception Score}
(IPS, 0--3 scale) assessed by the same GPT-4.1 judge
(see Appendix~\ref{app:ips} for the full rubric), decomposed into
\emph{trigger rate} (fraction of responses with IPS~$>$~0) and
\emph{perception depth} (mean IPS over triggered responses).

\section{Neuron-Level Analysis of the Intent--Safety Pathway}
\label{sec:neurons}

We hypothesize that the gap originates from an under-activated
neural pathway: intent detection neurons fire correctly, but the
subsequent pivot neurons---which should translate recognition into
a refusal trajectory---remain dormant under adversarial conditions.

\subsection{Localizing Intent Neurons}
\label{sec:intent_neurons}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
We operationalize malicious intent following the teleological
account of rational-action understanding
\citep{PLACEHOLDER_teleological_verify}: intent is the goal state
inferred from an action given a context.
Neurons sensitive to malicious intent should respond differently
to the \emph{same harmful action} under a harmful context
versus a legitimized context.

\paragraph{Context-differential scoring.}
For each harmful query~$A$ with original context~$C_{\rm ori}$,
we construct nine legitimized variants $\{w \oplus C_{\rm ori}\}$
(\eg, fictional novel, movie script, academic paper) and compute
the differential activation of neuron~$i$ in layer~$l$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Score}_i^{(l)} =
  \frac{
    \mathrm{TrimMean}_{w \in \mathcal{W}}
    \bigl[ f(A, C_{\rm ori}) - f(A,\, w \oplus C_{\rm ori}) \bigr]
  }{
    \sqrt{(\mu_{\rm benign}^{(l)})^2 + (\sigma_{\rm benign}^{(l)})^2}
    + \epsilon
  },
  \label{eq:intent_score}
\end{equation}
where $f(\cdot)$ denotes the neuron activation, the trimmed mean
discards the highest and lowest context variant to reduce noise,
and the denominator normalizes by the benign baseline statistics
computed on TextVQA (1{,}000 samples).
We apply Equation~\ref{eq:intent_score} to a mixed harmful corpus
of 195 samples from JailbreakV-28K, 54 from MMSafetyBench, and
50 from FigStep.

\paragraph{Layer selection.}
We compute the mean inter-layer similarity of neuron scores and
observe a sharp inflection between layers~13--16 (Figure~\ref{fig:layer_sim}),
indicating that intent representations crystallize in this range.
We designate layers~13--16 as the \emph{sensing layers}
$L_{\rm sensing}$ and select the top-$K$ neurons per layer by
$\mathrm{Score}_i^{(l)}$ as intent neurons
$\mathcal{I}^{(l)}$.

\paragraph{Validation.}
Activating $\mathcal{I}^{(l)}$ with a fixed scalar for all
samples replicates the intent-prompt effect:
IPS rises comparably (Table~\ref{tab:intent_val}).
Yet, consistent with Section~\ref{sec:gap}, ASR does not decrease.
Figure~\ref{fig:intent_dist} shows that intent-neuron activations
clearly separate harmful from benign samples, confirming their
selectivity.

\subsection{Localizing Safety Neurons: Pivot Neurons as the Key Bottleneck}
\label{sec:safety_neurons}

\paragraph{Integrated-gradient attribution.}
For safety neurons we use \emph{integrated gradients}
(IG)~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_ig_verify}, which measure the causal
contribution of each neuron to the model's refusal behavior.
For a harmful input $x$ and a zero-activation baseline $x'=\mathbf{0}$:
\begin{align}
  ig_i^{\rm harm}(x) &= x_i \int_0^1
    \frac{\partial F(\alpha x)}{\partial x_i} d\alpha,
  \label{eq:ig}\\
  IG_i^{\rm harm} &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\rm harm}|}
    \sum_{x \in \mathcal{D}_{\rm harm}} ig_i^{\rm harm}(x),
  \label{eq:ig_avg}\\
  \mathrm{Score}_i^{\rm safety} &=
    IG_i^{\rm harm} - \lambda\, IG_i^{\rm benign},
  \label{eq:safety_score}
\end{align}
where $F(\cdot)$ outputs the log-probability difference
$\log p(\text{``sorry''}) - \log p(\text{``sure''})$,
$\mathcal{D}_{\rm harm}$ is VLSafe (500 samples),
and $\mathcal{D}_{\rm benign}$ is Dolly-15K~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_dolly_verify}.
We select the top-5\% neurons per layer as safety neurons,
restricting the search to layers after the sensing layers and
before the final fluency layers~\citep{PLACEHOLDER_hidden_states_verify}.

\paragraph{Pivot neurons: the decisive component.}
Among the identified safety neurons, we observe a functionally
distinct subgroup whose top-activated tokens are \emph{linguistic
pivot} words:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  Layer & Pivot tokens (neuron indices) \\
  \midrule
  17 & \emph{but}, \emph{otherwise} (553, 5258) \\
  18 & \emph{instead} (9709) \\
  19 & \emph{unfortunately} (6683) \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
These neurons encode the \emph{turn} from compliant generation to
refusal---a crucial inflection that, as we show next, is the
primary bottleneck for safe behavior under jailbreak.

The remaining safety (refusal) neurons encode explicit
rejection vocabulary:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  Layer & Refusal tokens (neuron indices) \\
  \midrule
  18 & \emph{cannot} (8281) \\
  21 & \emph{hurt} (9616) \\
  22 & \emph{never} (9883) \\
  25 & \emph{sorry} (7605) \\
  26 & \emph{prevent} (7173, 10919) \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{\method: Adaptive Pivot Neuron Activation}
\label{sec:method}

The ablation in Section~\ref{sec:ablation} used fixed-value
activations applied uniformly to all samples.
Two problems prevent this from being a practical defense:
\textbf{(i) Uniformity}: a fixed boost over-activates pivot
neurons for benign queries and may under-activate them for
covert high-toxicity queries;
\textbf{(ii) Absolute magnitude}: raw activation magnitudes
conflate question complexity with toxicity (complex benign
questions also produce large activations).

\method addresses both by using the \emph{relative activation of
intent neurons}---their signal-to-noise ratio with respect to the
full hidden state---as a self-aware toxicity signal.

\subsection{Self-Aware Malicious Intent Score}

For token position~$t$ in layer~$l \in L_{\rm sensing}$, define
the \emph{per-layer intent SNR}:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_t^{(l)} =
  \frac{\mathbf{w}^{(l)} \cdot \mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}^{(l)}}^{(l)}(t)}
       {\|\mathbf{H}_t^{(l)}\|_2},
  \label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}^{(l)}}^{(l)}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}^{(l)}|}$
is the activation vector of intent neurons at token~$t$ in layer~$l$,
$\mathbf{H}_t^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the full hidden state,
and $\mathbf{w}^{(l)}$ are purity-weighted coefficients:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w}^{(l)} = \frac{\mathbf{s}^{(l)}}{\|\mathbf{s}^{(l)}\|_1},
  \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{s}^{(l)}$ being the vector of intent scores
$\mathrm{Score}_i^{(l)}$ (Equation~\ref{eq:intent_score}) for
neurons in $\mathcal{I}^{(l)}$.
Dividing by the full hidden-state norm converts the measure from
absolute activation strength to a \emph{signal-to-noise ratio},
making it invariant to question complexity
(Appendix~\ref{app:snr_analysis} provides a formal derivation).

We aggregate across sensing layers by taking the peak SNR per
layer and summing:
\begin{equation}
  \Phiharm = \sum_{l \in L_{\rm sensing}} \max_t\, \rho_t^{(l)}.
  \label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Threshold selection.}
Figure~\ref{fig:dist} shows that $\Phiharm$ is
bimodally distributed: harmful queries cluster high, benign queries
cluster low.
We set the activation threshold $\epsnoise$ at the crossing point
of the two empirical densities:
\begin{equation}
  \epsnoise^* = \arg\max_{\epsilon}
  \biggl[
    \int_\epsilon^\infty f_{\rm harm}(x)\,dx
    - \int_\epsilon^\infty f_{\rm benign}(x)\,dx
  \biggr],
  \label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
maximizing true-positive rate while controlling false activations
on benign inputs.

\subsection{Adaptive Boost Computation}

Given $\Phiharm$, the excess toxicity signal is
$\Delta\Phi = \max(0,\, \Phiharm - \epsnoise^*)$.
The boost applied to each safety neuron~$i$ in layer~$l$ at
generation step~$t$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Boost}_i^{(l)}(t) =
  \tanh(\Delta\Phi) \cdot \gamma_l \cdot s_i^{(l)} \cdot M(t),
  \label{eq:boost}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_l$ is a layer-depth scaling factor (deeper layers
receive higher weight), $s_i^{(l)}$ is the neuron purity score
(Equation~\ref{eq:intent_score}, reflecting localization
confidence), and $M(t)$ is a temporal mask:
\begin{equation}
  M(t) = \begin{cases}
    1 & t \leq t_{\rm pivot} \text{ (prefill + first refusal token)},\\
    0 & t > t_{\rm pivot},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:mask}
\end{equation}
which prevents over-refusal by deactivating the boost after the
model has committed to a refusal trajectory.
For intent neurons, the boost is instead applied throughout
(without masking) to maintain intent representation:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}^{(l)}}^{(l)}(t) \leftarrow
  \mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}^{(l)}}^{(l)}(t)
  + \rho_{(1)}^{(l)} \cdot \mathbf{s}^{(l)},
  \label{eq:intent_boost}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{(1)}^{(l)}$ is the peak-layer SNR.

The overall inference procedure is summarized in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:method}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \caption{\method: Adaptive Pivot Neuron Activation}
  \label{alg:method}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Query $(V, T)$; intent neurons
      $\{\mathcal{I}^{(l)}\}_{l \in L_{\rm sensing}}$;
      pivot/refusal neurons
      $\{\mathcal{S}^{(l)}\}_{l \in L_{\rm safety}}$;
      threshold $\epsnoise^*$; purity scores $\mathbf{s}$
    \STATE \textbf{// Prefill pass: compute intent SNR}
    \STATE Run model forward on $(V,T)$; collect
      $\{\mathbf{H}^{(l)}\}$
    \STATE Compute $\Phiharm$ via Equations~\ref{eq:snr}--\ref{eq:phi}
    \STATE $\Delta\Phi \leftarrow \max(0,\, \Phiharm - \epsnoise^*)$
    \STATE \textbf{// Boost intent neurons (full generation)}
    \FOR{$l \in L_{\rm sensing}$}
      \STATE Apply Equation~\ref{eq:intent_boost} to
        $\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}^{(l)}}^{(l)}$
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE \textbf{// Boost pivot + refusal neurons (masked)}
    \FOR{$t = 1, 2, \ldots$}
      \FOR{$l \in L_{\rm safety}$}
        \STATE Apply $\mathrm{Boost}_i^{(l)}(t)$
          (Equation~\ref{eq:boost}) to $\mathcal{S}^{(l)}$
      \ENDFOR
      \IF{model emits first refusal token}
        \STATE Set $M(t') \leftarrow 0$ for all $t' > t$
      \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE \RETURN Generated response
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Why SNR beats absolute activation.}
Figure~\ref{fig:snr_case} presents representative cases showing
that for (a) a low-toxicity complex benign query, absolute intent
activation is high despite no malicious intent, while the SNR
correctly stays low; and (b) a covert high-toxicity query with
hidden harmful intent, absolute activation is moderate, while the
SNR spikes---triggering the defense appropriately.
This validates our theoretical motivation (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Visualization of intent neurons.}
Figure~\ref{fig:neuron_dist} visualizes the spatial distribution
of intent neurons (Equation~\ref{eq:intent_score}) across layers
and neurons within each layer.
Neurons are densely concentrated in layers~14--16 and tend to
cluster around a subset of feed-forward intermediate dimensions,
consistent with the layer-localization result from
Section~\ref{sec:intent_neurons}.

\paragraph{The shortcut hypothesis.}
Our findings are consistent with a shortcut-learning account
of RLHF safety training.
During SFT/RLHF, end-to-end rewards incentivize the model to
emit refusal tokens in response to recognizable harm cues.
The resulting shortcut: match surface tokens $\to$ emit refusal
prefix.
This shortcut bypasses the deeper circuit (intent detection $\to$
pivot $\to$ refusal), which is never reinforced as an independent
pathway.
Adversarial attacks mask surface cues; the shortcut fails; the
deep path cannot compensate.
\method re-enables the deep path at inference time, without
requiring retraining, by directly activating the pivot neurons
that the deep path requires.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Model coverage.}
Our primary experiments are on LLaVA-1.5-7B; the generalization
experiments cover [N] additional models.
It remains to be verified whether the pivot-neuron bottleneck
exists in substantially different architectures (\eg, Mixture-of-Experts
or state-space models).

\textbf{Neuron localization cost.}
Localizing intent and safety neurons requires a one-time
forward/backward pass over a few hundred labeled samples per model.
This cost is incurred once and is amortized over all subsequent
inferences, but it does require access to a small safety dataset.

\textbf{Adaptive adversaries.}
We evaluate against existing jailbreak attacks.
An adaptive attacker who knows which neurons \method activates
could potentially craft attacks that suppress those neurons.
Investigating such adaptive threats is an important direction for
future work.

\textbf{Threshold calibration.}
The threshold $\epsnoise^*$ is calibrated on a held-out split of
the training-time harmful/benign distribution.
Distribution shift (\eg, novel attack types not seen during
calibration) may affect the threshold's precision.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We demonstrated the \emph{intent--safety gap}: enhanced malicious
intent awareness in LVLMs does not reduce ASR under jailbreak
attacks.
Through mechanistic analysis, we identified \emph{pivot neurons}---
neurons encoding critical linguistic transitions from
problem-recognition to refusal---as the primary bottleneck in the
intent-to-refusal pathway, a bottleneck that arises because
SFT/RLHF shortcut training never fully reinforces this deeper
pathway.
We proposed \method, a training-free inference-time mechanism that
uses intent neuron SNR as a self-aware toxicity signal to
adaptively activate pivot and refusal neurons, bridging the gap
while preserving utility on benign queries.
Our results suggest that future safety training procedures should
explicitly reward the full intent$\to$pivot$\to$refusal
pathway rather than only the refusal outcome.

\section{Intent Perception Score (IPS) Evaluation Rubric}
\label{app:ips}

The GPT-4.1 judge assesses IPS on a 0--3 scale:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{3 --- Explicit Hazard \& Consequence.}
    The model explicitly identifies the specific physical,
    medical, or legal danger, or states severe real-world
    consequences.
  \item \textbf{2 --- Generic Caution / Soft Guardrail.}
    The model expresses friction or states that the request is
    ``not appropriate'' or ``dangerous,'' but relies on generic
    warnings.
  \item \textbf{1 --- Shallow / Subconscious Awareness.}
    The model exhibits bare-minimum awareness: subconscious
    emotional shifts or opaque boilerplate refusals.
  \item \textbf{0 --- Total Blindness.}
    The model executes the request with zero friction.
\end{itemize}

The full judge prompt is included in the supplementary code.

\section{SNR Normalization: Formal Justification}
\label{app:snr_analysis}

Let $\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be a hidden state and
$\mathbf{h}_\mathcal{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}|}$ its
restriction to intent neurons.
The L2 norm $\|\mathbf{h}\|_2$ is a proxy for the
\emph{representational complexity} of the current token
(\eg, semantically rich tokens produce larger norms).
For a benign but complex query, both $\mathbf{h}_\mathcal{I}$
and $\|\mathbf{h}\|_2$ are large; their ratio
$\rho = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{h}_\mathcal{I} / \|\mathbf{h}\|_2$
remains moderate.
For a covert harmful query with hidden intent, $\mathbf{h}_\mathcal{I}$
is selectively elevated relative to $\|\mathbf{h}\|_2$, producing
a high $\rho$.
Formally, under a spherical Gaussian model of hidden states,
$\mathbf{E}[\rho \mid \text{benign}] = O(|\mathcal{I}|^{1/2}/d^{1/2})$
while $\mathbf{E}[\rho \mid \text{harmful}]$ is amplified by
the intent-neuron selectivity factor, providing a clear
statistical separation.
